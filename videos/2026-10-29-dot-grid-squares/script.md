# Can You Find All the Squares? | AMC 8-Style Level 5

## Intro

Twenty dots. How many squares can you make with them?

## The question

Here's the question.

We have twenty dots, in four rows of five.

Neighbouring dots are one unit apart.

How many squares have all four corners on dots?

And here's the twist: count squares of every size, including tilted ones.

Is it twenty, twenty-four, twenty-six, thirty, or forty?

## Solution 1: sort by size

Welcome back! Our first strategy is to sort the squares by size.

Let's start with the upright ones. They sit straight, like floor tiles.

The smallest are one by one. The grid is four squares across, and three down.

So that's four times three. Twelve!

Next, two by two squares. Let's slide one around the grid.

It fits in three spots across, and two spots down. That's six.

Three by three squares fit in just two spots.

Can we go bigger? A four by four square needs four units of height, but our grid is only three tall. So, no.

Twelve, plus six, plus two. That's twenty upright squares.

Twenty! Are we done? Not yet. Remember the tilted squares?

Here's the smallest tilted square. Each side goes one across, and one down.

It fits snugly inside a two by two box.

We already found six two by two boxes. Each one holds one of these. So, six more!

Now a bigger tilted square. Each side goes two across, and one down.

It fits inside a three by three box. And look, it has a mirror twin!

Two three by three boxes, with two tilted squares each. That's four more.

A bigger tilted square would need a four by four box. And that won't fit.

So we have twenty, plus six, plus four. That's thirty squares!

## Solution 2: the box trick

Now here's a faster way. I call it the box trick.

Every square, upright or tilted, fits snugly inside an upright box. It touches all four sides.

Look at this two by two box. It holds the box itself, plus one tilted square. That's two squares.

Now a three by three box. Its corners step along the sides, from dot to dot.

Zero steps. One step. Two steps. With three steps, we are back to the box! So, three squares in one box.

See the pattern? A two by two box holds two snug squares. A three by three box holds three.

So we just count the boxes, and multiply by their size.

Twelve one by one boxes, times one, is twelve.

Six two by two boxes, times two, is twelve.

Two three by three boxes, times three, is six.

Twelve, plus twelve, plus six. Thirty again!

When should you use the box trick? When the grid gets big. It saves a LOT of drawing.

## Common traps

Now, let's look at the wrong answers. Each one is a trap.

Twenty is what you get if you forget the tilted squares completely.

Twenty-four means you missed the six small tilted squares.

Twenty-six means you missed the four big tilted ones. And forty is just too many!

So here's a great habit. Sort by size, and always ask: did I check for tilted ones?

## Bigger grid: the answer

Let's check. This grid is four units across, and four down.

One by one boxes: sixteen of them. Times one, that's sixteen.

Two by two boxes: three across and three down, so nine. Times two, that's eighteen.

Three by three boxes: four of them. Times three, that's twelve.

Last, one big four by four box. It hides four squares!

Sixteen, plus eighteen, plus twelve, plus four. That's fifty!

If you found all fifty squares, amazing work!

Here's the big idea to remember.

Sort squares by the box they fit in snugly. A three by three box holds three snug squares.

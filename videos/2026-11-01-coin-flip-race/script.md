# Who Gets More Heads? 5 Coins vs 7 | AMC 10-Style Level 4

## Intro

Bob flips seven coins, and his friend Ann flips five. Who gets more heads?

Bob has two extra coins, so he should usually win. But how often, exactly?

## The question

Here's the question. Our friend Ann flips five fair coins, and Bob flips seven fair coins.

What is the probability that Bob gets more heads than Ann?

Here are the five choices. Take a good look.

## What are we asked?

So what do we know? Every coin is fair: heads and tails are equally likely.

We want Bob's heads to be MORE than Ann's heads. A tie does not count.

Comparing two piles of coins is tricky. Let's see if we can turn it into ONE count.

## Solution 1: flip Ann's coins

Welcome back!

Look at Ann's coins. Her heads are five minus her tails, like three heads and two tails.

So Bob wins when his heads beat five minus Ann's tails.

Move Ann's tails across: Bob's heads plus Ann's tails is more than five, so at least six.

Now flip all of Ann's coins over. Her tails turn into heads.

Flipped fair coins are still fair coins, so every chance stays the same.

So Bob wins exactly when twelve fair coins show at least six heads.

Twelve coins can land two to the twelfth ways. That's four thousand ninety-six.

Sort them into three piles: fewer than six heads, exactly six, and more than six.

The outer piles are mirror images. Swap every head and tail, and one turns into the other.

The middle pile is twelve choose six: pick which six coins show heads.

Multiply twelve down to seven, then divide by six down to one. That's nine hundred twenty-four.

Four thousand ninety-six minus nine hundred twenty-four is three thousand one hundred seventy-two.

The two outer piles share that evenly: one thousand five hundred eighty-six each.

At least six heads means the middle pile plus the top pile.

Nine hundred twenty-four plus one thousand five hundred eighty-six is two thousand five hundred ten.

So the chance is two thousand five hundred ten over four thousand ninety-six.

Divide both by two: one thousand two hundred fifty-five over two thousand forty-eight. That's Choice D!

## Solution 2: fair game plus a tiebreak

Here's another way. Hide one of Bob's coins for now: six coins against five.

Now Bob has more heads than Ann, or more tails than Ann. Exactly one of the two!

More of neither, and he'd have five coins at most. More of both, and he'd need seven.

Heads and tails are equally likely, so Bob wins this six-coin game exactly half the time.

Now bring back coin seven. It only matters when Bob's six coins tie with Ann.

To find a tie, flip Ann's coins again. A tie means exactly five heads among eleven coins.

Eleven choose five is four hundred sixty-two, out of two to the eleventh, two thousand forty-eight.

Coin seven lands heads on half of those ties: two hundred thirty-one more wins.

One half is one thousand twenty-four over two thousand forty-eight. Add two hundred thirty-one.

One thousand two hundred fifty-five over two thousand forty-eight. Same answer!

When would you pick this way? With just one extra coin, it gives one half right away.

## Common traps

Now the traps! First, let's write all five choices over two thousand forty-eight.

Choice A is the top pile alone. That's what you'd get if exactly six heads didn't count.

Choice B, one half, is right for six coins against five. Bob's seventh coin adds the tiebreak wins.

Choice E adds the pile with exactly five heads. That's what you'd get if a tie counted as a win.

Choice C, five hundred seventy-five over one thousand twenty-four, is sneakier.

That's what you'd get if Bob's first five coins raced Ann's, and his two spares only broke ties.

But Bob can also come back from one behind, when both spares land heads!

So here's a great habit: always ask if a tie counts. MORE than, or at least?

## Three against five: the answer

Let's check. Flip Ann's three coins over. Bob needs at least four heads among eight coins.

Eight coins land two to the eighth ways: two hundred fifty-six. Eight choose four is seventy.

The rest, two hundred fifty-six minus seventy, is one hundred eighty-six: ninety-three each.

At least four heads: seventy plus ninety-three is one hundred sixty-three.

The answer is one hundred sixty-three over two hundred fifty-six. If you got it, amazing work!

## Recap

Flip one player's coins over. Fair coins stay fair, so the chances don't change.

Then a race for more heads becomes one count, for one big pile of coins.

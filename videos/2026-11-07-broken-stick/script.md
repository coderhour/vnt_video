# Break a Stick Twice: What Are the Odds? | AMC 10-Style Level 4

## Intro

Snap a stick in two random places, and you get three pieces.

Is the longest piece less than twice the shortest? Here's a hint: it's rarer than you might think.

Hi, I'm Vantee! Let's turn this stick puzzle into a picture.

## The question

Here's the question: a stick is cut at two points, chosen at random along its length. That makes three pieces.

What is the probability that the longest piece is less than twice as long as the shortest piece?

Here are the five choices. Take a good look.

## What are we asked?

Let's make the stick sixty units long. Any length gives the same chance.

Pieces of sixteen, twenty and twenty-four win. Twenty-four is less than two times sixteen, thirty-two.

But ten, twenty and thirty loses. Thirty is NOT less than two times ten, which is twenty.

## Solution 1: map the cuts

Welcome back! Call the first cut x and the second y: every pair of cuts is a dot in a sixty by sixty square.

Random cuts give a random dot. So the chance is a share of the square's area, three thousand six hundred.

Above the diagonal, the second cut is further right. The pieces are x, y minus x, and sixty minus y.

Below the diagonal is a mirror copy. So let's work in the top half. Its area is one thousand eight hundred.

Which piece is shortest? Three lines, where two pieces are equal, cut the top half into six slices.

All three lines meet at one point, where every piece is twenty.

Take the slice where left is shortest and right is longest. Its base is thirty, and its height is twenty.

So its area is half of thirty times twenty. That's three hundred.

No piece is special, so all six slices have the same area. Six times three hundred is one thousand eight hundred!

In our slice, we win when the right piece is less than two times the left piece.

Where left equals middle, the border has right equal to two lefts. That's one, one, two parts of sixty: fifteen, fifteen, thirty.

Where middle equals right, right is still two lefts. That's one, two, two parts of sixty: twelve, twenty-four, twenty-four.

So the winning part is a small triangle, from the equal point to these two spots.

Let's find its area with a box trick. The box is eight wide and ten tall, so its area is eighty.

Cut off the three right triangles around ours. Half of five times ten is twenty-five.

Half of four times eight is sixteen. Half of three times six is nine.

That's fifty in all. So the winning triangle is eighty minus fifty. That's thirty!

Every slice works the same way. Six winning triangles make a hexagon of one hundred eighty.

The bottom half is a mirror copy. So the chance is one hundred eighty out of one thousand eight hundred.

That's one over ten! Choice C.

## Solution 2: the walking shortcut

Here's a faster way, with no box at all. Stay in our slice, and start at the equal point.

Walk along an edge to the far corner. The shortest piece shrinks steadily, from twenty down to zero.

On edge one, we already found the border: fifteen, fifteen, thirty.

The shortest piece shrank five out of twenty. That's one over four of the way.

On edge two, we already found the border: twelve, twenty-four, twenty-four.

It shrank eight out of twenty. That's two over five of the way.

Shrink two sides of a triangle toward one corner, and its area shrinks by both fractions, multiplied.

One over four, times two over five, is one over ten. Much faster! Pick this when the winning part sits in a corner.

## Common traps

Now, the traps.

A, one over twenty, is what you'd get if you forgot the mirror copy below the diagonal.

B, one over twelve, is what you'd get if one whole slice counted as a win.

D, one over six, is the chance of just one ordering. It forgets the twice-as-long rule.

Choice E, one over four, is the famous chance that the three pieces can make a triangle. Our test is stricter!

## Three times: the answer

Let's walk again, with a stick one hundred five long.

The equal point has three pieces of thirty-five. On edge one, ratio one, one, three gives twenty-one, twenty-one, sixty-three.

The shortest piece shrank fourteen out of thirty-five. That's two over five of the way.

On edge two, ratio one, three, three gives fifteen, forty-five, forty-five.

It shrank twenty out of thirty-five. That's four over seven of the way.

Two over five, times four over seven, is eight over thirty-five!

## Recap

The big idea: two random cuts make a random dot in a square.

Every ordering of the pieces gets an equal slice. So find the winning share of just one slice.

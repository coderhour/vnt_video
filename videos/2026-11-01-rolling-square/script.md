# How Far Does the Rolling Corner Travel? | AMC 10-Style Level 4

## Intro

A little square tumbles around a big one. Most kids miss a sneaky detail at the corners.

Hi, I'm Vantee! Let's get rolling.

## The question

A square with side two is fixed in place.

A square with side one sits on top of it, and their left sides line up.

The small square rolls clockwise around the big one, without sliding.

Each move turns it about a corner touching the big square, until another side lies flat.

It stops when it first gets back to where it started. How long is the path of the marked corner?

Here are the five choices. Take a good look.

## What are we asked?

Which corner do we follow? The one that started on the big square's top left corner. Call it P.

So P is the small square's BOTTOM left corner. Keep your eye on P.

Each move tips the square over a pivot corner. We add up how far P travels on every move.

## Solution 1: one move at a time

Welcome back! Our strategy: follow the square one move at a time.

First move: the square tips over its bottom right corner. That corner is the pivot.

It makes a quarter turn, ninety degrees, and lands flat on top again.

P is one away from the pivot, so P swings along a quarter circle of radius one.

A full circle of radius one is two pi long, so a quarter of it is pi over two.

Now the small square sits at the big square's top right corner, and tips over it.

After a quarter turn, look! It only touches at one point. No side lies flat yet.

So it keeps going. It needs a half turn, one hundred eighty degrees, to land flat on the right side.

How far is P from this pivot? P is across the diagonal of the small square: root two.

So P sweeps half a circle of radius root two. That's root two times pi.

Down the right side, another quarter turn. The corner P is one away again, so that's pi over two.

Next, the big square's bottom right corner. Surprise: the pivot IS P! The square flips, and P stays put.

That move adds zero. That's half a lap, and the other half repeats the same four moves.

Quarter turn, radius one. Half turn, root two. Quarter turn, radius one. Half turn, zero.

P is back at its start, and the square is home. Done!

Now add it up. Four quarter circles of radius one: four times pi over two, which is two pi.

Two half circles of radius root two: two times root two pi.

And the two flips around P add nothing, zero.

Total: two plus two root two, all times pi. That's Choice C!

## Solution 2: glue arcs into circles

Here's a slicker way to see the total. Glue the arcs together into whole circles!

Four quarter circles of radius one make one full circle of radius one. That's two pi.

Two half circles of radius root two make one full circle of radius root two. That's two root two pi.

One circle plus the other: two pi plus two root two pi. Same answer!

When would you pick this way? When the arcs come in matching sets, like here.

## Common traps

Now the wrong answers. Each one is a different slip.

Choice A, two plus root two, all times pi, comes from treating each corner move as a quarter turn.

But after a quarter turn the square only touches at a point. It must go on to a half turn.

Choice B, four pi, is what you'd get if you used radius one, not root two, for those half circles.

But at the top right corner, P is across the diagonal from the pivot, not along a side.

Choice D, four plus root two, all times pi, is what you'd get if you followed the wrong corner.

That's how far the small square's TOP left corner travels. Our P started at the bottom left.

Choice E, four plus two root two, all times pi, is what you'd get if the flips around P had radius one.

But P is the pivot there, so its radius is zero. It doesn't move at all.

Wrong angle, wrong radius, wrong corner, forgotten zero. Four slips to watch for!

## Variant: the answer

Let's check. The center is half a diagonal from every corner: root two over two.

So on every move, the center is the same distance from the pivot. No zeros this time!

Now count the turning: four quarter turns and four half turns.

Four times ninety plus four times one hundred eighty is one thousand eighty degrees.

That's three full turns. So the center travels three full circles of radius root two over two.

Each circle is root two pi long, so the path is three root two pi. Fantastic work!

## Recap

In a rolling problem, list every move: its pivot and its angle.

Each point travels an arc whose radius is its distance from the pivot. Sometimes that's zero!

## Solution 1: count the gaps

Welcome back! Strategy one: count the gaps between the squares.

Between one and four, two numbers survive. Between four and nine, four survive.

Between nine and sixteen, six survive. The gaps go two, four, six, eight, growing by two.

After k squared, the gap holds two k numbers. The next square is k squared plus two k plus one.

Now add up the gaps. Two, then six, then twelve, then twenty.

That's one times two, two times three, three times four, four times five. See the pattern?

Try forty-four gaps: forty-four times forty-five is one thousand nine hundred eighty. Just under!

So k gaps hold k times k plus one numbers.

Forty-five gaps would be forty-five times forty-six, two thousand seventy. Too many!

Those are all the survivors below forty-five squared, which is two thousand twenty-five.

Hop over it. Then two thousand twenty-six takes spot one thousand nine hundred eighty-one.

We want spot two thousand twenty-six. That's forty-six more than spot one thousand nine hundred eighty.

So count forty-six numbers past two thousand twenty-five.

Two thousand twenty-five plus forty-six is two thousand seventy-one! Choice C.

The gap after forty-five squared holds ninety numbers, so we never hit another square.

## Solution 2: add back the squares

Here's a second way. Think about what the knocked-out squares do.

Each square below the answer pushes it one spot along. So add HOW MANY squares there are.

First guess: two thousand twenty-six, plus the squares up to it.

Forty-five squared is two thousand twenty-five. So we skip forty-five perfect squares.

Two thousand twenty-six plus forty-five is two thousand seventy-one.

Now check! Did we jump over a new square on the way?

The next square is forty-six squared, two thousand one hundred sixteen. We're safe!

Here's a shortcut for test day. Find the perfect square nearest to the spot number, and add its root.

The nearest square to two thousand twenty-six is forty-five squared. Add forty-five. Same answer!

Numbers between k squared and the next square have spots near k squared. And k squares came before.

When would you pick this one? When the clock is ticking. Just remember to check.

## Common traps

Now the traps. Here's a handy test: the spot is the number, minus how many squares come before it.

Choice A, two thousand sixty-nine, minus forty-five, lands in spot two thousand twenty-four. Too early.

B, two thousand seventy, is what you'd get if you forgot that one is a perfect square too.

Test it: take away forty-five, and you land in spot two thousand twenty-five. Too early.

D, two thousand seventy-two, is what you'd get by adding forty-six instead of forty-five.

Test it: take away forty-five, and you land in spot two thousand twenty-seven. Too late.

E, two thousand one hundred sixteen, is forty-six squared. It isn't even in the list!

So here's a great habit. After you add the squares, check that you didn't jump over a new one.

## Spot one thousand: the answer

Thirty-one squared is nine hundred sixty-one. That's thirty-one perfect squares below one thousand.

First guess: one thousand plus thirty-one, which is one thousand thirty-one.

Now check. Thirty-two squared is one thousand twenty-four. We jumped right over it!

So push one more spot. The answer is one thousand thirty-two!

Check again: thirty-three squared is one thousand eighty-nine, safely past it.

The shortcut agrees: the nearest square to one thousand is thirty-two squared. Add thirty-two.

## Recap

Big idea: the answer is its spot, plus HOW MANY squares sit below it. Then check for one more!

Or use the shortcut: add the root of the perfect square nearest to the spot number.

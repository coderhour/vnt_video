# Two Buttons, One Target: Fewest Presses | AMC 8-Style Level 4

## Intro

A calculator with just two buttons. Can you reach ninety in as few presses as possible?

Most kids start pressing forward and get lost. There's a much smarter direction.

Hello, I'm Vantee! Let's crack this calculator.

## The question

Here's the question.

A calculator shows three.

It has only two keys. One adds one to the number on the screen.

The other doubles the number on the screen.

What is the fewest number of key presses needed to make the screen show ninety?

Here are the five choices. Take a good look.

## What are we asked?

So what do we know? We start at three, and we want to end at ninety.

Each press either adds one, or doubles.

We don't just want a way to get there. We want the FEWEST presses.

## Solution 1: work backwards

Welcome back! Here's the strategy: work backwards, from ninety down to three.

Going backwards, doubling becomes halving, and adding one becomes taking one away.

Halving is the big move, so halve whenever you can. If the number is odd, take one away first.

Ninety is even, so cut it in half. That gives forty-five.

Forty-five is odd, so take one away: forty-four. Halve it: twenty-two. Halve again: eleven.

Eleven is odd, so take one away: ten. Halve it: five.

Five is odd, take one away: four. Now four is even, but half of four is two, and that's below three!

So take one away instead. Four minus one is three. We made it!

Read it forwards from three: every arrow is one key press.

Now count the arrows, not the numbers. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight.

Eight presses. That's Choice C!

## Solution 2: plan the doubles

Can anyone beat eight? Let's plan the doubles, and see why not.

Five doubles is too many. Three doubled five times is ninety-six, already past ninety.

With four doubles, three becomes forty-eight. We still need forty-two more.

Here's the trick. A plus-one pressed early gets doubled later, like a snowball.

A plus-one before all four doubles is worth sixteen. Before three doubles, eight. Then four, two, and one.

Make forty-two from those values: sixteen plus sixteen plus eight plus two. That's four adds.

Can three plus-one presses do it? The biggest three are sixteen, sixteen, sixteen: forty-eight, too big.

The next biggest is sixteen, sixteen, eight. That's forty, too small. Nothing in between!

So we need four doubles and four plus-one presses. That makes eight, and no fewer!

And it matches our chain: two plus-one presses at the start, one after the first double, one before the last.

With fewer doubles, it only gets worse. Three doubles would need nine adds. That's twelve presses.

When would you pick each way? Work backwards to find a short path fast.

Plan the doubles when you need to be sure nothing is shorter.

## Common traps

Now let's look at the wrong answers. Each one is a trap.

Choice D, nine, counts the numbers in the chain. But the presses are the arrows between them, one fewer.

Choices A and B, six and seven, are too few. We showed you need four doubles and four adds.

Choice E, ten, can reach ninety, but it isn't the fewest. Two presses wasted!

So here's a great habit. Count the steps, not the stops.

## Aim for 100: the answer

Let's work backwards. One hundred, halve: fifty. Halve: twenty-five.

Twenty-five is odd, take one away: twenty-four. Then cut it in half three times: twelve, six, three!

Count the arrows: six presses.

Check: five doubles take three to ninety-six, and one snowball add-one makes the last four. Six presses!

Here's the sneaky part. The bigger target took fewer presses. If you got six, amazing work!

## Recap

Here's the big idea to remember.

To reach a target with doubling and adding one, work backwards.

Cut it in half when it's even, unless that drops below the start.

Otherwise, take one away.

And count the presses, the arrows, not the numbers.

# When Is the Clock Angle a Whole Number? | AMC 8-Style Level 4

## Intro

At one o'clock, the clock hands make a nice whole angle: thirty degrees.

One minute later, it's twenty-four and a half degrees. That is not a whole number.

## The question

Here's the question.

Look at every time from one o'clock to one fifty-nine, one minute apart.

At how many of these times is the smaller angle between the hands a whole number of degrees?

## Solution 1: a formula

Welcome back! The minute hand makes a full turn, three hundred sixty degrees, in sixty minutes.

That's six degrees every minute.

The hour hand moves one number, thirty degrees, in sixty minutes. That's half a degree a minute.

Now let's build a formula. Call the minutes past one o'clock m.

At one o'clock, the hour hand points at the one. That's thirty degrees past the twelve.

After m minutes, it has moved half a degree for each minute. So it's at thirty plus half of m.

The minute hand starts at the twelve. After m minutes, it's at six m.

Every minute, the minute hand gains six minus a half. That's five and a half degrees per minute.

So the gap between the hands is thirty minus five and a half m.

Let's test it at twenty past one. Thirty minus one hundred ten is minus eighty.

A gap can't be negative, so the angle is eighty degrees.

At forty past one, the gap is one hundred ninety. That's more than one hundred eighty.

So the smaller angle is three hundred sixty minus that: one hundred seventy degrees.

Now the big question. When is thirty minus five and a half m a whole number?

Thirty is whole. So we need five and a half times m to be whole.

Five and a half times an even number is whole. Five and a half times two is eleven.

An odd number leaves a half. Five and a half times three is sixteen and a half.

Three hundred sixty minus a whole number is whole too. So it's whole exactly when m is even.

Let's count the even minutes. Pair them up: zero and one, two and three, and so on.

Sixty minutes make thirty pairs, with one even minute in each pair.

So the answer is thirty. That's D!

## Solution 2: watch each hand

Here's a quicker way. Look at each hand on its own.

The minute hand moves six degrees a minute, so it always sits on a whole degree.

The hour hand starts at thirty and moves half a degree a minute.

On even minutes, it's on a whole degree. On odd minutes, it's on a half, like thirty and a half.

Whole minus whole is whole. But a whole number minus something and a half is never whole.

So the angle is whole every other minute. Thirty times!

When would you pick this way? When you want a fast answer without a formula.

## Common traps

Now, let's look at the wrong answers. Each one is a trap.

Sixty counts every minute, without checking whether the angle is whole.

Twenty-nine counts the even minutes from two up to fifty-eight, and forgets one o'clock itself.

Twelve is what you would get if you only counted times when the minute hand points right at a number.

Fifteen is what you'd get if you counted only even angles. But odd ones, like nineteen, count too.

So here's a great habit. Check the very first time, and test an even minute AND an odd minute.

## New hour: the answer

At two o'clock, the hour hand is at sixty degrees. So the gap is sixty minus five and a half m.

First, the angle must be whole, so m must be even.

Five and a half times an even minute gives eleven, twenty-two, thirty-three: multiples of eleven.

Sixty is a multiple of five. So we need a multiple of eleven that is ALSO a multiple of five.

That's zero, then fifty-five, then one hundred ten, and so on.

Fifty-five is five and a half times ten, so they come every ten minutes.

Three hundred sixty minus a multiple of five is one too. So m is zero, ten, twenty, up to fifty.

That's six times! Check ten past two: sixty minus fifty-five is five degrees.

If you found six, amazing work!

## Recap

Here's the big idea.

Write the gap between the hands as a formula in the minutes: thirty minus five and a half m.

Then ask which minutes make it whole. Here, every even minute works.

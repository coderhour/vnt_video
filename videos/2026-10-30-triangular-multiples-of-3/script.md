## Solution 1: track the remainders

Welcome back! Our strategy: forget the big numbers. Track the remainder when you divide by three.

One leaves a remainder of one. Three leaves zero, so it's a multiple.

Each number is the one before, plus a bit more. So we can just add up the remainders!

Six is three plus three. Adding a three never changes the remainder. So six leaves zero.

Ten is six plus four. Four leaves one. Zero plus one is one, so ten leaves one.

Fifteen is ten plus five. Five leaves two. One plus two is three, which leaves zero!

Twenty-one is fifteen plus six. Adding six changes nothing, so it leaves zero.

See the pattern? One, zero, zero. Then one, zero, zero again.

Why does it keep repeating? Every three steps, we add three numbers in a row.

Like four, five and six. Together they make fifteen, which is five threes.

Three numbers in a row always make a multiple of three. So the remainder comes right back.

So the list comes in groups of three spots. The remainders go one, zero, zero.

Every group holds exactly two multiples of three, in its second and third spots.

One hundred is thirty-three threes, plus one. That's thirty-three full groups, and one extra spot.

Thirty-three groups, times two each, gives sixty-six multiples.

The extra spot is spot one hundred. It starts a new group, so it leaves one. Not a multiple!

So the answer is sixty-six. That's D!

## Solution 2: use the formula

Here's a second way. Each number is a sum. Spot four holds one plus two plus three plus four.

Draw it as a staircase of dots. Then add a second copy, flipped upside down.

Together they make a rectangle, four by five. That's twenty dots, so one staircase is ten.

The same works for any spot. Spot n holds n times n plus one, all divided by two.

When is that a multiple of three? The top, n times n plus one, is twice our number.

Twice a number is a multiple of three exactly when the number is. So let's just check the top.

Two whole numbers multiply to a multiple of three only when one of them is a multiple of three.

First, n itself: three, six, up to ninety-nine. That's thirty-three spots.

Next, n plus one: n is two, five, up to ninety-eight. That's thirty-three more.

Neighbors can't both be multiples of three. So it's thirty-three plus thirty-three: sixty-six again!

When would you pick this way? When you know the formula, it gives the rule for every spot at once.

## Common traps

Thirty-three counts only spots three, six, nine, and so on. It forgets spots like two, five and eight.

Thirty-four is what you'd get counting spots one, four, seven, up to one hundred. They all leave one!

Fifty is what you'd get guessing every other number. But the pattern repeats every three, not two.

Sixty-seven is what you'd get if you counted the extra spot too. But spot one hundred leaves one.

So here's a great habit. Find the cycle, count the full groups, then check the leftovers one by one.

Twenty-six groups, times two each, gives fifty-two multiples.

Don't chase the big numbers. Track the remainders, and find the rule for which spots work.

The pattern is one, zero, zero. So in every full group of three spots, two are multiples.

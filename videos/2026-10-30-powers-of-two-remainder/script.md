# The Remainder of a Giant Sum of Powers of Two | AMC 8-Style Level 5

## The question

Here's the question.

Add one, plus two, plus four, plus eight, and keep doubling.

The last number is ninety-nine twos multiplied together.

What is the remainder when this sum is divided by seven?

Is it zero, one, two, four, or six?

## What are we asked?

So what are we really looking for?

The remainder is what's left over after taking out all the sevens.

Take twenty, for example. Two sevens make fourteen, and six is left over.

Now let's count the terms. The little number on top is the power.

Going down, each power halves: four, then two, then one. So one has power zero.

So the powers go from zero up to ninety-nine.

That's one, plus ninety-nine more. One hundred terms, not ninety-nine!

The total is far too big to work out. We need a pattern.

## Solution 1: find the cycle

Welcome back! Our strategy: look for a pattern in the remainders.

One, two and four are smaller than seven. They leave one, two and four.

Eight is seven plus one. So eight leaves one!

Sixteen is fourteen plus two. It leaves two.

Thirty-two is twenty-eight plus four. It leaves four.

See the cycle? One, two, four, and then back to one.

Here's why. Each power is double the last, so its remainder doubles too.

When it gets too big, we take out a seven. Double four is eight, and eight leaves one.

Now add one cycle. One plus two plus four is seven. Nothing left over!

So let's split our hundred terms into groups of three.

Every group leaves one, two and four. That's seven, a perfect fit.

Ninety-nine terms make thirty-three groups. Together, they leave nothing.

One term is left on its own. Its power is ninety-nine.

Powers zero, three, six, and so on, start a cycle. They all leave one.

Ninety-nine is a multiple of three. So the last term leaves one.

So the whole sum leaves a remainder of one. The answer is B!

## Solution 2: the doubling trick

Here's a clever second way. Let's add one more one to our sum.

One plus one is two. Two plus two is four. Four plus four is eight.

It keeps snowballing, all the way to the end.

The total becomes one hundred twos multiplied together.

So our sum is that big number, minus one.

Now, eight leaves one. So when we only care about sevens, times eight acts like times one!

Ninety-nine twos make thirty-three eights. So that part leaves one.

One more two doubles it. So one hundred twos leave two.

Take away one, and the remainder is one. Same answer!

When would you pick this way? When every term doubles, this trick turns the sum into one power.

## Common traps

Now, let's look at the wrong answers. Each one is a trap.

Zero is what you get with only ninety-nine terms. Don't forget the last one!

Two is the remainder of the big power, before taking away one.

Four and six can never happen. Look at the running totals.

One, three, seven, fifteen, thirty-one, sixty-three. They only leave one, three, or zero.

So here's a great habit. Count your terms carefully, starting from power zero.

## Divide by five: the answer

Let's check. Divided by five, the powers leave one, two, four, three, and then one again.

This cycle is four long. One plus two plus four plus three is ten. That's two fives!

From power zero to power fifty, there are fifty-one terms.

Fifty-one is twelve fours, plus three. So forty-eight terms make twelve full groups. They leave nothing.

Three terms are left over, with powers forty-eight, forty-nine and fifty.

Forty-eight is a multiple of four, so power forty-eight starts a new cycle.

They leave one, two and four. That adds up to seven, and seven leaves two!

If you found a remainder of two, amazing work!

## Recap

Here's the big idea to remember.

Find the cycle of remainders. Check if one full cycle adds up to a multiple of the divisor.

Then deal with the leftover terms.

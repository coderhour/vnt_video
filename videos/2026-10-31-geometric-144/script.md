How many three-number chains like this end at one hundred forty-four?

## The question

Here's the question.

How many increasing geometric sequences of three positive whole numbers, a, b, c,

have c equal to one hundred forty-four?

Here are the five choices.

## What are we asked?

Geometric means we multiply by the same ratio each step, from a to b, then from b to c.

Increasing means a is less than b, and b is less than c.

And watch out: the ratio doesn't have to be whole.

Sixty-four, ninety-six, one hundred forty-four uses a ratio of three over two.

## Solution 1: focus on the middle

Welcome back! Let's focus on the middle number, b.

Same ratio means b over a equals c over b. So b times b equals a times c.

With c equal to one hundred forty-four, a equals b squared,

divided by one hundred forty-four.

Try a few. Six or eighteen give fractions. But twenty-four works: a is four.

Here's the trick. One hundred forty-four is twelve squared.

So a is b over twelve, squared.

If b over twelve weren't whole, its square wouldn't be whole either.

Three over two squared is nine over four. Not whole!

But a IS whole. So b over twelve must be whole.

Call it k. Then b is twelve k, and a is k squared.

k equals one gives one, twelve, one hundred forty-four.

k equals two gives four, twenty-four, one hundred forty-four. And so on.

At k equals twelve, all three numbers are one hundred forty-four.

That's not increasing, so we stop at eleven.

k can be one through eleven. That's eleven sequences, Choice C!

## Solution 2: the ratio as a fraction

Here's another way. Write the ratio as a fraction, p over q, in lowest terms.

p is bigger than q, since the chain increases.

Going backward from c, a is one hundred forty-four times q squared, over p squared.

q squared shares no factor with p squared, so p squared must divide one hundred forty-four.

The squares above one that divide it are four, nine, sixteen, thirty-six and one hundred forty-four.

So p is two, three, four, six or twelve.

For each p, q is any smaller number that shares no factor with p.

That's one, plus two, plus two, plus two, plus four. Eleven again!

When would you pick this way? When c isn't a perfect square. This method works for any c.

## Common traps

Now the traps. Choice D, twelve, includes the chain where all three are one hundred forty-four.

But that doesn't increase!

Choice E, fourteen, is what you'd get if any factor of one hundred forty-four could go first.

But if a is two, then b squared is two hundred eighty-eight. That's not a square!

Choices A and B, nine and ten, are what you'd get if you skip fractions like twelve over eleven.

Count every letter k from one to eleven, and none slip away.

## Ending at 72: the answer

Let's check. Again, b squared equals seventy-two times a.

Seventy-two is thirty-six times two, and thirty-six is a square.

Then b over six, squared, equals two times a. So two times a is a perfect square.

Two times a is even, so the square is even: four, sixteen, thirty-six, sixty-four, one hundred.

So a is two, eight, eighteen, thirty-two or fifty.

The chains run from two, twelve, seventy-two, up to fifty, sixty, seventy-two.

The next one would be seventy-two, seventy-two, seventy-two. Not increasing!

So there are five sequences. If you got five, amazing work!

## Recap

Here's the big idea to remember.

In a geometric sequence, the middle times itself equals the outside two multiplied.

So a times c must be a perfect square.
